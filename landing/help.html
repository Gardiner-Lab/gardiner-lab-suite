How an experiment runs through Trellis Tools

One construct, followed from an idea to a homozygous line and the paper figure, through every tool in the suite. The point of keeping them in one place is that each step remembers the one before it, so at the end you can still answer: where did this line come from, who made it, and what was done to it? Before you start

You sign in once, at the suite's front door, and every tool knows who you are. Three things shape what you see:

  • Your account — your own notebook, your own saved work.
  • Your lab — its stocks, seeds, plasmids and equipment are shared with everyone in it. Your lab has a short lab code, chosen once by its lead, which begins every code it ever issues: with the code GL, GL-G-0042 is that lab's forty-second glycerol stock.
  • Your chair group — the other labs beside yours. They can see your stocks and seeds, but not change them. That is what makes borrowing an antibody or crossing with a neighbour's mutant possible without handing over your records.

Everything below assumes nothing more than that.

1 Design the construct MoClo Library Tool

1

Find the parts, or add them

The lab's Golden Gate parts are already there: promoters, coding sequences, terminators, each with its 4 bp fusion sites, who contributed it and where the tube is. Anything new you add once, properly, and everyone has it.

2

Check the set before you order anything

The compatibility checker finds the two things that actually ruin an assembly: overhangs that clash or leave a gap, and an internal BsaI or BpiI site that the reaction would cut. It names the part at fault.

3

Assemble it on screen

Choose the backbone and the parts in order; the tool joins them through their fusion sites and shows the plasmid you are about to make, every part marked. Save it, and send it to the reaction calculator for the mix, scaled to the number of reactions.

2 Check it over Plasmid Editor

Both tools share one library, so the assembled plasmid opens straight in the editor. Here you can look at it the way you would any sequence: its map, its features, what cuts it and where, a virtual gel of the digest you plan to run, and the primers you will need. If the lab already owns a sequencing primer that binds it, the editor says so — searching by sequence finds it before anyone orders another.

If the work is not MoClo-based at all — a Gibson, a restriction ligation, a Gateway LR — this is where you simulate it, and the product you save carries the features of both parents.

3 Build it, and write down what you did Lab Notebook

Press Start today and the day's page opens, filed under its month and year. Bring the reaction in from MoClo and its parts and volumes arrive as a table you can tick off as you pipette. Paste in the gel photo, click the wells you used and name each lane, so the picture explains itself later.

Then the step that ties it all together: type @ and link the plasmid. The link survives renaming, and the plasmid now knows this page exists.

4 Confirm the clone Plasmid Editor Lab Stocks

Upload the sequencing reads against the plasmid. Each is aligned and shown with its chromatogram, its quality and every mismatch, and the coverage tells you what the reads reached.

When the reads say the clone is right, confirm it. Two things happen at once: the plasmid is marked confirmed, with who confirmed it and from which reads; and its glycerol stock appears in Lab Stocks with the next code, already linked to the sequence that proved it. Fill in the strain and which box the tube is in, print the label, and freeze it.

This is the moment most labs lose: the tube goes in the freezer and the sequence stays on a laptop. Here the tube and the proof are one record, and if eLabJournal is switched on, the stock is copied there with its box and position.

5 Into plants Seed Bank

1

The line

A line is a genetic identity, not a generation: 35S:tRFP-4 stays one line whether you are looking at its T1 or its T3. Say which plasmid it carries, and it reaches back to the editor and to the glycerol stocks of that plasmid.

2

The seeds

Each physical bag is a seed lot with its own code and label: where it came from, how much is left, its generation. Dip the plants, collect the T1 seed, and that bag is a lot of its own.

3

Sowing

Sow from the lot rather than writing on a tray: each plant gets a code and a number, knows which lot it came from, where it is growing and under what treatment. Print the pot labels; a phone camera on the QR code opens the plant.

6 Growing, and what you see Seed Bank Booking

The Growing page is every plant still alive, by where it grows. Select a whole sowing or open it and pick individual plants, then score a trait across them, move them, change their treatment, or mark the ones that died.

Score phenotypes against named traits, so they can be compared between plants, lines and treatments later, with photos and 3D scans attached. Booked the confocal to take them? That booking shows at the top of your notebook day, so a picture can always be traced to the microscope that took it.

7 The next generation Seed Bank

Harvest the plant you want to keep, and its seeds become a new lot — one that remembers the plant it came off, which remembers the lot it was sown from, back to the beginning. Sow it, test it on selection plates, record the segregation, and when a line is homozygous say so on the lot.

An independent transformant is a new genetic identity, so found a line from that plant. Later generations of it are not new lines; they are lots of the same one.

8 A cross with another lab Seed Bank

The lab next door has the mutant you want to cross into. Because you are in the same chair group, you can see their lines and plants without them handing anything over. Cross your plant with theirs: the F1 seed lot records both parents, one from each lab, and the new line's pedigree reaches into both.

Nobody has given up control. Their records stay theirs to change; you can only read them.

9 Following the thread back

Two years later, someone finds a bag labelled GL-S-0042 and asks what it is. Open it and press Family tree.

The family tree: the whole history on one page

It is on every line, seed lot and plant, and on every plasmid and glycerol stock. Whichever you start from, one page gathers what would otherwise be four tools and an afternoon:

  • The construct — the plasmid the line carries, its length, whether its sequence was confirmed, and a link that opens it in the Plasmid Editor.
  • Its glycerol stocks — straight from Lab Stocks, with the clone, the strain, which box the tube is in and whether it is still there.
  • Where it came from — its parents, then the chain of seeds back through every generation: lot → plant → lot → … → the bag in your hand.
  • What came of it — everything grown from it since, as a tree.
  • The notebook pages about each of those steps, listed beside it, as far as you are allowed to open them.

Start from a plasmid instead and the same page answers the other question: every line ever made with it. A very large tree shows its first few hundred steps, and you open a record further down for the rest.

The thread, link by link
  • Part → plasmid: the assembly keeps the parts it was made from.
  • Plasmid → glycerol stock: confirming a clone makes the stock.
  • Plasmid → line: the line says what it carries.
  • Lot → plant → lot: every sowing and every harvest, in both directions.
  • Anything → notebook page: @ links one way, and the record lists the pages that mention it the other.
  • Plant → plant material: the DNA tube in the freezer knows which plant it came from.

Sharing, between people and between labs

What your lab shares by default

Stocks, seeds, plasmids, protocols and equipment belong to the lab, not to a person: anyone in it sees and changes them. That is the point — when someone leaves, their tubes do not leave with them.

What stays yours

Your notebook is yours alone until you share it. Share a single page with a colleague, or a whole notebook with your lab; a lab lead can be given the notebook of someone in their lab. The protocols library is shared with the lab as a matter of course, so there is one copy of each method, improved over time — and a day page keeps the version it used, so improving a protocol never rewrites your history.

What the chair group sees

The other labs beside yours see your stocks and seeds read-only: enough to find a shared antibody, borrow a chemical or cross with a mutant, never enough to change your records. Equipment is shared deliberately — whoever adds a microscope chooses which labs or chair groups may book it.

Beyond the suite

Where the university keeps its own inventory, Lab Stocks copies your stocks to eLabJournal and takes back what changed there, so the institutional record stays right without anyone typing twice.

The habits that make it work

  1. Record it as it happens. Every tool is quickest at the bench, on the day. Nothing here can reconstruct what nobody wrote down.
  2. Link instead of retyping. @ in the notebook, the plasmid on the line, the plant on the sample. Each link is what somebody later will follow.
  3. Let the tools make the records. Confirming a clone makes its stock; harvesting a plant makes the lot; sowing makes the plants. Entering them by hand is how a thread breaks.
  4. Write the code on the tube. A record with no code on the thing it describes helps nobody.
  5. Mark, don't delete. A dead plant, an empty bag, a finished tube: say so. What others descend from cannot be deleted, and should not be.
  6. Use the same words. Trait names, places, treatments: pick from what is already there, so things can be compared later.

What is kept, and where

Everything lives on your own server, in plain databases with the photos and files inside them, backed up each night to wherever your backups are kept. Nothing is sent anywhere else, apart from the copy in eLabJournal when that is switched on.

Nothing is thrown away quietly: every notebook page keeps its earlier versions, every record keeps a history of who changed what, deleted pages can be brought back, and a deleted record is hidden rather than erased so the things pointing at it keep working.
